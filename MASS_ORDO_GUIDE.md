# The Order of the Mass in the Sehling Church Orders

This guide compares the **order of the Mass** (*ordo missae*, *Ordnung des Amts*, *Ordnung des
Nachtmahls*) in the evangelical church orders printed in Emil Sehling's *Die evangelischen
Kirchenordnungen des XVI. Jahrhunderts*. Other guides in this collection treat the parts of the
Mass one by one. This guide puts them back together. It asks:
- **The whole ordo.** What sequence of parts did each order prescribe, from the opening to the
  dismissal? Which orders kept the shape of the Roman Mass, and which built a new service around
  the sermon?
- **Families.** Which orders copied which? Where do the main types of evangelical Mass come
  from?
- **The ordinaries.** What happened to the fixed parts (the Kyrie, Gloria, Creed, Sanctus and
  Agnus Dei), and to the fixed dialogues and formulas around them (the salutation, the Preface
  dialogue, the Pax, the *Ite missa est*)? In particular:
  - Was the Kyrie sung three, four or nine times? Were the old seasonal Kyrie chants kept?
  - Who intoned the Gloria and the Creed? Which creed was sung?
  - Was the Sanctus sung with or without the Benedictus, and where did it fall?
  - How many times was the Agnus Dei sung, and how did it end?
  - What took the place of the *Ite missa est*?
- **The service without communicants.** What remained of the Mass when nobody came to the Lord's
  Table?

**How the guide is laid out.**
- §1 summarizes the findings, and §2 sets out the scope, the method and what was not found.
- §3 sets the baselines: the Roman ordo, and Luther's *Formula missae* (1523) and *Deutsche
  Messe* (1526).
- §4 describes the families of evangelical ordo, and §5 compares them in a table.
- §§6–13 take the ordinaries and fixed formulas in order: Kyrie (§6), Gloria (§7), salutation,
  collect and Pax (§8), Creed (§9), Preface and Sanctus (§10), the Lord's Prayer and the Words
  of Institution (§11), Agnus Dei (§12), and the dismissal and blessing (§13).
- §14 deals with the Sunday service when nobody communicated.
- §15 is a concordance of the orders quoted.

**Conventions**

- **Quotations.** Every quotation is Sehling's text as it stands in the `eko.db` database built
  from the digitized edition.
  - Footnote numbers, sigla, folio markers and interleaved apparatus are left out.
  - Sehling's own supplied words in square brackets are kept, as are "[!]", "[sic]" and his
    other signs.
  - "[Noten]" marks and the like are omitted.
  - Spelling and punctuation are as printed, including plain OCR errors.
  - "[…]" marks an omission.
  - Every quotation has been checked against the database text, allowing for OCR noise: at least
    90 per cent of its words must be found there in order. Its page has been checked against the
    edition's page markers.
  - Each quotation is preceded by an HTML comment, invisible when rendered, that names the
    `eko.db` document it was checked against.
- **Translations.** The English is formal-equivalence, in the idiom of the Authorized Version:
  - Word order and clause structure are kept where English allows.
  - Liturgical names and stock phrases (Kyrie, Gloria, *Et in terra*, *Patrem*, Sanctus,
    *Benedicamus*) are kept in their customary Latin or English forms.
  - Words the translation supplies are in square brackets.
- **Citations.** Citations are in the form "Sehling volume, page".
  - The half-volumes of the edition are written 6/1, 6/2, 7/1, 7/2.1, 7/2.2, 17/1, 20/1 and so
    on, following the digitized volumes.
  - Each order is named by place, title and date as they appear in Sehling's running heads.
    Where a passage is Sehling's own introduction or apparatus, this is said.
- **Ordo shorthand.** In the tables, the parts are abbreviated: Intr. (introit or the hymn in
  its place), Ky. (Kyrie), Gl. (Gloria), Coll. (collect), Ep. (epistle), Grad. (gradual,
  alleluia, sequence or hymn after the epistle), Gosp. (gospel), Cr. (creed), Serm. (sermon),
  Exh. (exhortation to the communicants), Pref. (Preface), San. (Sanctus), PN (Lord's Prayer),
  Verba (Words of Institution), Agn. (Agnus Dei), Comm. (distribution), Thanks. (postcommunion
  collect or thanksgiving), Bl. (blessing).

## 1. Summary of findings

**1. Two baselines, not one** (§3). Luther left two Masses, and the orders descend from both.
- **The *Formula missae* (1523)**: the Latin Mass with the offertory and canon cut and the Words
  of Institution sung in the Preface; the ordinaries kept "with various melodies for the various
  seasons"; *Benedicamus* for *Ite missa est*.
- **The *Deutsche Messe* (1526)**: German hymns for the introit and the chants; the Kyrie three
  times "and not nine"; no Gloria; a paraphrase of the Lord's Prayer and an exhortation for the
  Preface; the bread and cup given separately after their words.

**2. Seven families of ordo** (§4).
- **Saxon** (Wittenberg 1533, Saxony 1539, 1580): the Latin school Mass with German pieces
  grafted on; Preface only on feasts or not at all.
- **Bugenhagen's northern Mass** (Braunschweig 1528 to Mecklenburg 1552): Latin and German side
  by side; Preface, Sanctus and exhortation optional; "after the Amen nothing more is sung".
- **Franconian** (Nürnberg 1533, Hof 1592): no Preface; the epistle and gospel read as chapters
  in course; **the Sanctus after the Words of Institution**, then the Lord's Prayer and the Pax.
- **Prussian** (1525): the Words of Institution sung in the Preface; Kyrie in three tongues;
  Agnus Dei twice.
- **The Latin frame** (Brandenburg 1540, Pfalz-Neuburg 1543, the Palatinate 1546, Breslau 1557):
  Preface dialogue, Pax and threefold Agnus kept; Breslau even kept *Ite missa est*.
- **Upper German** (Württemberg 1553, Strasbourg 1598): the sermon service with the Supper
  added; no Kyrie, Gloria, Preface, Sanctus or Agnus.
- **Reformed** (Palatinate 1563, Bentheim-Tecklenburg 1588): the Supper form read at the table,
  the Apostles' Creed, the breaking of bread.

**3. Kyrie: three, four or nine; never seven** (§6.1). Luther's "three times and not nine"
became the parish norm. Nine survived for feasts and choirs (Wittenberg 1533; Pomerania 1542). A
fourfold Kyrie (Kyrie, Christe, Kyrie, Kyrie) comes from Müntzer (1524) and survives in
Calenberg-Göttingen 1542 and the Hessian *Agende* of 1574. Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel (1543)
allows "three times, four times or nine times" in figured music "at the schoolmaster's
pleasure", but three plainly "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost".
No sevenfold Kyrie is found.

**4. The seasonal Kyrie chants survived** (§6.2). The orders with Latin schools kept the
medieval cycle by name: *dominicale*, *angelicum*, *paschale*, *Fons bonitatis*, *Magne Deus*,
*summum*, *minus summum*, *apostolicum*, *de martyribus*, *de virginibus* (Buxtehude 1526;
Pomerania 1542; Nördlingen 1544; Pfalz-Zweibrücken 1557; Hof 1592, with the matching Gloria,
Sanctus and Agnus "of the season").

**5. Gloria: the festal companion of the Kyrie** (§7). Not after "the plain Kyrie" (Wittenberg
1533); "at times and not always" (Pomerania 1535). Where kept, the priest intoned, the scholars
sang *Et in terra* "and no more", the people sang "Allein Gott", and the scholars and organ
finished the Latin (Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel 1543).

**6. Creed: three layers, and two creeds** (§9). The priest intoned *Credo in unum Deum*, the
scholars sang the Latin *Patrem*, and the people "Wir glauben all" (Wittenberg 1533 and most
Saxon and northern orders). Prussia (1525) forbade the priest's intonation. Which creed (§9.4):
- **Nicene only**: the Saxon, Bugenhagen and Franconian orders, in Latin, in German verse by
  verse (Braunschweig 1528, Hamburg 1529), or as "Wir glauben".
- **Apostles' only**:
  - Strasbourg 1524, sung after the sermon, "the great *Patrem* … sung by some";
  - the Goslar foundation 1534;
  - the Upper German pulpit (Württemberg 1553);
  - the Reformed Supper (Frankfurt's French church 1554, the Palatinate 1563).
- **A free choice**: Hesse 1574 (the Apostles' Creed, Luther's paraphrase, or the Nicene);
  Kurland 1570.
- **By occasion**:
  - Hesse 1566: the Apostles' Creed every Sunday, the Nicene or Athanasian on the feasts of
    Christ;
  - Rothenberg 1618: "Wir glauben" on Sundays, the Apostles' Creed on other feasts;
  - Prussia 1568 and Feuchtwangen 1563: the Latin Nicene and the German creed on alternate
    Sundays.

**7. The Preface was optional; the Sanctus moved** (§10). The Preface was required in only a few
orders, festal or optional in most, absent in the *Deutsche Messe*, Nürnberg, Albertine Saxony
1580 and Württemberg. The Sanctus stood in five places: before the Verba, after the Verba with
or without a Preface, during the distribution, or nowhere. The Latin Sanctus kept its
*Benedictus*; Luther's "Jesaia dem Propheten" lost it; the Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel Trinitarian
Sanctus kept it with the Hebrew *Hosianna*.

**8. The Lord's Prayer and the Verba in seven sequences** (§11): the Lord's Prayer before the
Verba, after them, paraphrased in the exhortation, sung as a hymn before the confession, or said
inside a prayer before the creed. The bread and cup were given separately after their own words
in the *Deutsche Messe*, Prussia 1544 and the Saxon villages.

**9. Agnus Dei: three, two or one** (§12). Three, as of old (Brandenburg 1540; Regensburg;
Luther's German "Christe du Lamm Gottes" with "Gib uns deinen Frieden" the third time). **Two**
in Prussia 1525 and Livonia 1533, "for brevity's sake", the first ending *miserere nobis* and
the second *dona nobis pacem*. **Once** in the Albertine *Cellische Ordnungen* (1545). It stood
before, during or after the distribution.

**10. No *Ite missa est*; Benedicamus or nothing; Aaronic, Trinitarian or both** (§13).
- **The *Ite missa est***: kept only at Breslau (1557, beside the *Benedicamus*) and in the
  Ansbach visitation of 1536.
- **The *Benedicamus***, in its place:
  - in Latin, in the *Formula missae* and the Franconian, Pfalz-Neuburg, Regensburg, Hohenlohe,
    Henneberg and Transylvanian orders;
  - in German, in the early German Masses (Müntzer, Erfurt, Lippe, Worms, Strasbourg) and at Hof
    in 1592.
- **Neither**: the *Deutsche Messe* and most Saxon, Bugenhagen and Upper German orders end with
  the collect and the blessing.
- **The blessing**:
  - the Aaronic blessing is the rule;
  - Worms and Strasbourg (1524) and Regensburg (1542) used a Trinitarian blessing alone;
  - Strasbourg put both together;
  - Brandenburg-Nürnberg (1533) offered four forms (Numbers 6, Psalm 67 and two Trinitarian), a
    set copied by a dozen orders;
  - Prussia (1525) sang Psalm 67 on feasts and the Aaronic blessing on ordinary days;
  - Strasbourg (1598) closed the sermon with the Trinitarian blessing and the Supper with both.

**11. Without communicants, no Mass** (§14). The service stopped after the sermon with a psalm,
a collect and the blessing (Pomerania 1535; Mecklenburg 1552; Saxony 1580), or fell back to an
office of psalm, chapter, creed and suffrages (Transylvania 1547). Hof kept a Mass-shaped daily
service for its schoolboys, with the Kyrie, Sanctus and Agnus of the season and no communion.

## 2. Scope, sources and method

### 2.1 Sources and searches

The guide draws on the full text of the Sehling edition in `eko.db`. Two searches were made:
- **Full ordos.** Every passage was found in which, within about 5,000 characters, the order
  names at least thirteen of the parts of the Mass (introit, Kyrie, Gloria, collect, epistle,
  gradual or sequence, gospel, creed, sermon, exhortation, Preface, Sanctus, Lord's Prayer,
  Words of Institution, Agnus Dei, distribution, thanksgiving, blessing). These passages, about
  forty in all, were read in full, and the representative ones are compared in §§4–5.
- **The ordinaries.** The corpus was searched for:
  - the Kyrie near the words for numbers (*drei*, *dreimal*, *neun*, *vier*, *sieben*, *ter*,
    *novies*, *semel*, *einmal*), and for runs of printed acclamations, which were counted;
  - the named Kyrie chants (*dominicale*, *angelicum*, *paschale*, *Fons bonitatis*, *Magne
    Deus*, *summum*, *apostolicum*, *de martyribus* and the rest);
  - the Agnus Dei near the same number words and near *dona nobis pacem*, *miserere nobis*, *gib
    uns deinen Frieden*;
  - *Benedictus qui venit*, *Osanna*, *Hosianna*;
  - *Ite missa est*, *Benedicamus*;
  - *symbolum apostolicum*, *Nicenum*, *Athanasii* near the Mass and its parts.

Pages were checked against Sehling's running heads, which often correct the database's record
headings. One attribution made earlier in this collection was corrected in the course of the
work: the Breslau account of the Sunday Mass and of the hours of the Passion is a report of
1557, not an order of 1550 (Sehling 3, pp. 404–405), and [`OFFICES_GUIDE.md`](OFFICES_GUIDE.md)
has been amended accordingly.

### 2.2 What was not found

- **No sevenfold Kyrie.** The counts in the corpus are three, four and nine (§6.1). No order
  prescribes seven or six acclamations.
- **No order that kept the offertory or the canon** as the priest's prayer of sacrifice. What
  the Latin-frame orders kept is described in
  [`CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md`](CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md).
- **No evangelical Mass without communicants** (§14), except the Hof schoolboys' daily exercise,
  which has no consecration.
- **No fixed Lutheran ordo.** Even within a family the orders allow the minister to drop the
  Gloria, the Preface, the Sanctus or the exhortation "when it will grow too long". The tables
  give the full form each order prescribes; few churches will have sung all of it every Sunday.

## 3. The baselines: the Roman ordo and Luther's two Masses

### 3.1 The Roman ordo in outline

**What the reformers inherited.** The orders measure themselves against the late medieval Roman
Mass, which they call *die messe*, *das ampt* or *die hohe messe*. In outline it ran:
1. the priest's preparation at the altar (*Introibo*, Psalm 43, *Confiteor*);
2. introit, Kyrie (nine acclamations), Gloria;
3. salutation and collect(s);
4. epistle, gradual, alleluia or tract, sequence;
5. gospel, Nicene Creed (intoned by the priest, continued by the choir);
6. offertory and offertory prayers, the secret;
7. Preface dialogue (*Dominus vobiscum*, *Sursum corda*, *Gratias agamus*), Preface, Sanctus
   with Benedictus;
8. the canon, with the Words of Institution said silently and the elevation;
9. Lord's Prayer with its introduction (*Praeceptis salutaribus*) and embolism (*Libera nos*),
   the fraction, the Pax (*Pax Domini sit semper vobiscum*), Agnus Dei (three times, the third
   ending *dona nobis pacem*);
10. communion, communion chant, postcommunion;
11. *Ite missa est* (or *Benedicamus Domino* when there was no Gloria), blessing.

The sermon had no fixed place in the Roman Mass. Every evangelical order gave it one.

### 3.2 *Formula missae*, 1523: the Latin Mass purged

**Keep the ordinaries, cut the sacrifice.** Luther's Latin order of 1523 kept almost everything
before the creed "because it is ours and free". It cut the offertory and the canon, and moved
the Words of Institution into the Preface. The ordinaries stayed, sung by the choir "with
various melodies for the various seasons". **Wittenberg, Luther, *Formula missae*, 1523**
(Sehling 1, p. 5):

<!-- doc 2 -->
> Secundo, kyrieleison, ut hactenus celebratum est, variis melodiis pro diversis temporibus
> amplectimur cum sequenti hymno angelico ‘Gloria in excelsis, tamen in arbitrio stabit
> episcopi, quoties illum omitti voluerit.

Secondly, the Kyrie eleison, as it hath been celebrated hitherto, with various melodies for the
various seasons, we embrace, together with the angelic hymn following, *Gloria in excelsis*; yet
it shall stand in the bishop's judgment how often he will have it left out.

The rest of the order is described in [`CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md`](CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md),
§3. In outline (Sehling 1, pp. 5–6):
- introit, Kyrie, Gloria, one collect, epistle;
- a gradual of two verses with the alleluia; no sequences but *Grates nunc omnes*, *Sancti
  Spiritus* and *Veni sancte Spiritus*, at the bishop's pleasure;
- gospel, the Nicene Creed ("not displeasing" if sung, but at the bishop's choice), and the
  sermon either after the creed or before the introit;
- the preparation of bread and wine "during the creed or after" (*sub symbolo vel post*), in the
  accustomed manner;
- *Dominus vobiscum*, *Sursum corda*, the Preface, and the Words of Institution sung in the
  Preface tone;
- the Sanctus, with the elevation during the Benedictus;
- the Lord's Prayer, without the *Libera nos*, the fraction or the commixture, then at once *Pax
  Domini*, "a kind of public absolution";
- the communion during the Agnus Dei; the postcommunion *Quod ore sumpsimus* or *Corpus tuum*;
- *Benedicamus Domino* for *Ite missa est*, and the blessing.

The last item is Luther's replacement for the dismissal. **Wittenberg, Luther, *Formula missae*,
1523** (Sehling 1, p. 6):

<!-- doc 2 -->
> Loco ‘Ite missa’ dicatur: ‘Benedicamus domino’, adiecto (ubi et quando placet) alleluia in
> suis melodiis. Vel ex vespertinis ‘Benedicamus’ mutuentur. VIII. Benedictio solita detur. Vel
> accipiatur illa Numeri 6. quam ipse dominus digessit, d.: ‘Benedicat nos dominus et custodiat
> nos, ostendat nobis faciem suam et misereatur nostri, convertat dominus faciem suam ad nos et
> det nobis pacem.’ Vel illa psal. 96.: ‘Benedicat nos deus deus noster, benedicat nos deus et
> metuant eum omnes fines terrae. Amen.

In the place of *Ite missa* let *Benedicamus Domino* be said, with alleluia added (where and
when it pleaseth) in its melodies; or let the *Benedicamus* be borrowed from Vespers. Eighthly,
let the accustomed blessing be given. Or let that of Numbers 6 be taken, which the Lord himself
set in order, saying: "The Lord bless us and keep us, the Lord show us his face and have mercy
upon us, the Lord turn his face unto us and give us peace." Or that of Psalm 96 [67]: "God, even
our own God, shall bless us; God shall bless us, and all the ends of the earth shall fear him.
Amen."

### 3.3 *Deutsche Messe*, 1526: the German Mass for the people

**Three Kyries, German hymns, the Verba with the distribution.** The *Deutsche Messe* made a
different shape, for the parishes. A German psalm or hymn stood for the introit; the Kyrie was
cut to three acclamations; the Gloria was gone; a German hymn followed the epistle and "Wir
glauben all" the gospel; the Preface was replaced by a paraphrase of the Lord's Prayer and an
exhortation; the Words of Institution were sung, the bread given after the words over the bread
and the cup after the words over the cup, with German hymns during each; and the service ended
with a collect and the Aaronic blessing (Sehling 1, pp. 14–16). Of the Kyrie it says.
**Wittenberg, Luther, *Deutsche Messe und ordnung gottis diensts*, 1526** (Sehling 1, p. 14):

<!-- doc 3 -->
> Darauf! kyrie eleison, auch im selben thon, drei mal und nicht neun mal, wie folget. Kyrie
> eleison.

Thereupon the Kyrie eleison, also in the same tone, three times and not nine times, as
followeth: Kyrie eleison.

The German Sanctus "Jesaia dem Propheten" and "Christe du Lamm Gottes" belong to the
distribution and its close (§§10, 12; [`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §§12,
15).

## 4. The families of evangelical ordo

**Seven shapes.** The orders fall into families. Within each, later orders copy earlier ones,
often word for word. The families differ less in what they cut than in where they put what they
kept: the sermon, the Preface and Sanctus, the Lord's Prayer and the Words of Institution.

### 4.1 The Saxon Mass: Latin and German together

**Wittenberg and the Albertine orders.** The Wittenberg town order of 1533, Duke Henry's order
of 1539 and the Albertine *Kirchenordnung* of 1580 keep the Latin Mass of the *Formula missae*
for the school and graft the German pieces of the *Deutsche Messe* on to it. The common pattern:
- introit (Latin, or a German psalm), Kyrie, Gloria with the Latin *Et in terra*;
- a German collect, the epistle sung or read in German facing the people;
- an alleluia, sequence or German hymn;
- the gospel in German; the priest intones *Credo in unum Deum*, the scholars sing the Latin
  *Patrem*, and the people "Wir glauben all";
- the sermon; then, for the communicants, the paraphrase of the Lord's Prayer and the
  exhortation from the *Deutsche Messe* (Saxony 1539, 1580), or the Lord's Prayer sung
  (Wittenberg 1533);
- the Words of Institution sung in German;
- the distribution, with the Latin Agnus Dei and the German Sanctus, "Jesus Christus unser
  Heiland", "Gott sei gelobet" and Psalm 111;
- "Christe du Lamm Gottes", a German collect, the Aaronic blessing.

The Preface survives here only for the feasts. At Wittenberg "the Preface may well often be left
out, that we may the more diligently attend to the Lord's Supper, for that alone is the matter"
(Sehling 1, p. 704). Duke Henry's order allows a Latin Preface "on the feasts, and now and then
on Sundays" (Sehling 1, p. 274). The Albertine order of 1580 has none (Sehling 1, pp. 368–369).
On the Saxon Kyrie and Creed see §§6, 9.

### 4.2 Bugenhagen's northern Mass

**Braunschweig, Hamburg, Lübeck, Pomerania, Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel, Mecklenburg.**
Bugenhagen's orders (Braunschweig 1528, Hamburg 1529, Lübeck 1531, Pomerania 1535, Denmark 1537,
Schleswig-Holstein 1542, Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel 1543) and the Mecklenburg order of 1552 make
the Mass of the northern towns. Its marks:
- a confession and absolution, or a prayer at the altar, before the introit (Mecklenburg 1552,
  Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel 1543: [`MASS_PRELIMINARIES_GUIDE.md`](MASS_PRELIMINARIES_GUIDE.md),
  §4);
- Kyrie and Gloria in Latin or German, the Gloria "at times and not always" (Pomerania 1535);
- the creed intoned by the priest, the Latin *Patrem* sung "*dominicaliter*, in full", then "Wir
  glauben" (Pomerania 1535, Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel 1543, Mecklenburg 1552);
- the sermon, with the general prayer at its end;
- a Preface and Sanctus that are allowed but not required;
- the exhortation to the communicants, the Lord's Prayer and the Words of Institution sung
  together;
- the distribution with "O Lamm Gottes", "Jesus Christus", "Gott sei gelobet" or Psalm 111;
  "Christe du Lamm Gottes" after it; a collect and the Aaronic blessing.

The Pomeranian order puts the Preface, the Sanctus and the exhortation at the minister's
discretion, and allows him to go straight from the sermon hymn to the Lord's Prayer "as is also
done at Wittenberg". **Pomerania, *Kirchenordnung*, 1535** (Sehling 4, p. 341):

<!-- doc 1856 -->
> Denne singet me de prefatio latinisch överst nicht alle tidt, sunder went me will, sunderlick
> to den hogen festen, mit dem sanctus latinisch eder düdesch, unde dar na list men de
> exhortation dat sacrament betrepende mit korte. Lange singent dar etlike prestere lust to
> hebben, schal ut orsake vormeeden werden, dat sulke christlike unde heelsamen ceremonien dem
> volke lüstich bliven, unde nicht vordreetlick werden, dat me ock dat volk vormane, gern dar to
> blivende bet tor lesten benedictien eder segen. Dar umme wen dat volk na der predikie dat
> düdesche leed gesungen hefft, mach wol de prester to tiden unde vakene dat andere alto male
> staen laten, ock des exhortatio eder confessio, dat dit nicht to lank unde vordreetlick werde,
> unde lieven balde an: Lat uns beden, Vader unse etc., alse ock to Wittemberch wert geholden.

Then the Preface is sung in Latin, but not always, rather when one will, especially at the high
feasts, with the Sanctus in Latin or German; and thereafter the exhortation concerning the
sacrament is read, briefly. Long singing, wherein some priests have pleasure, shall for this
cause be avoided: that such Christian and wholesome ceremonies may remain pleasant to the people
and not grow wearisome, and that the people also be exhorted to remain there gladly until the
last benediction or blessing. Therefore, when the people after the sermon have sung the German
song, the priest may at times and often let all the rest stand over, even the exhortation or
confession, that this be not too long and wearisome, and begin straightway: Let us pray, Our
Father, etc., as is also kept at Wittenberg.

The Mecklenburg order of 1552 is the fullest of the family, and gives the Latin text and notes
of the creed and the Preface (§§9, 10).

### 4.3 The Franconian Mass: Nürnberg and Brandenburg-Ansbach

**A Mass without a Preface.** The Brandenburg-Nürnberg order of 1533 and its descendants (the
Nürnberg agendas, Hof 1592) keep the Latin ordinaries for the schools but make the communion its
own action, "the order of the Lord's Supper", after the sermon. Its marks:
- the priest says a *Confiteor* privately and reads the introit, the Kyrie and the *Et in
  terra*, which the scholars sing in Latin or the people in German;
- *Dominus vobiscum* and one or more German collects;
- the epistle and gospel read as chapters in course, "the first chapter of the epistle to the
  Romans" and so on, not the Sunday pericopes ([`PROPERS_GUIDE.md`](PROPERS_GUIDE.md), §5);
- the Credo sung in Latin by the scholars, or "Wir glauben" by the people; the sermon;
- no Preface: the exhortation, then the Words of Institution sung;
- **the Sanctus after the Words of Institution**, then the Lord's Prayer with its old Latin
  introduction, and the Pax;
- the distribution during the Agnus Dei; collect; *Benedicamus*; one of several blessings.

**Brandenburg-Nürnberg, *Kirchenordnung*, 1533** (Sehling 11, pp. 196–197):

<!-- doc 270 -->
> Darnach folgt das Sanctus lateinisch oder teutsch. Darauf alsbald: Oremus preceptis
> salutaribus moniti et divina institutione formati audemus dicere: Pater noster. […] Darnach
> als bald: Pax Domini sit semper vobiscum etc. oder aber teutsch, wie es hernach notiert ist

Thereafter followeth the Sanctus in Latin or German. Thereupon straightway: *Oremus, praeceptis
salutaribus moniti et divina institutione formati, audemus dicere: Pater noster.* […] Thereafter
straightway: *Pax Domini sit semper vobiscum*, etc., or else in German, as it is noted
hereafter.

The order of Hof (1592) keeps the Franconian shape, but moves the Lord's Prayer and the Pax
before the Words of Institution, and sings the Sanctus and Agnus Dei of the season during the
distribution (Sehling 11, pp. 425–426).

### 4.4 The Prussian Mass of 1525: the Words of Institution in the Preface

**The *Formula missae* in German.** The Prussian articles of 1525 follow the *Formula missae* in
setting the Words of Institution inside the Preface, sung in German, with the elevation, the
Sanctus after it, and the Pax as an absolution. They add three peculiarities: the Kyrie sung in
Greek, Latin and German, the creed not intoned by the priest, and the Agnus Dei sung only twice
(§§6, 9, 12). **Prussia, *Artikel der ceremonien und anderer kirchen ordnung*, 1525**
(Sehling 4, p. 32):

<!-- doc 1832 -->
> Volgt die prefation, welche der priester deütsch singet samt den evangelischen worten der
> gebenedeiung oder consecration oder brod und wein (den offertorium, secret, canon minor und
> maior werden notwendig ausgelassen). Auf die volendeten prefation und consecration gibt man
> ein zeichen mit der schellen, darnach so bald elevirt der priester beideteil des sacraments
> ane mittel nacheinander. Unter des und so bald die prefation samt der evangelischen
> benediction aus ist, singet der chor sanctus deütsch oder lateinisch nach gelegenheit, mit
> wenig noten.

Then followeth the Preface, which the priest singeth in German, together with the evangelical
words of the blessing or consecration of bread and wine (for the offertory, the secret, the
lesser and greater canon are of necessity left out). When the Preface and consecration are
ended, a sign is given with the bell; then straightway the priest elevateth both parts of the
sacrament, one after the other, without delay. Meanwhile, and as soon as the Preface together
with the evangelical benediction is ended, the choir singeth the Sanctus in German or Latin as
occasion serveth, with few notes.

The Livonian towns took over the Prussian order at Wolmar in 1533 (Sehling 5, pp. 15–16).

### 4.5 The Mass that kept the Latin frame

**Brandenburg, Pfalz-Neuburg, the Palatinate before 1556.** A group of orders of the 1540s kept
more of the Latin Mass than Wittenberg did: the Preface dialogue, the Pax, the Agnus Dei three
times, even the old prayers before communion. The Brandenburg order of 1540 and the
Pfalz-Neuburg order of 1543 are treated in
[`CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md`](CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md), §§12, 14. The interim order of the
Palatinate (1546) even kept the tail of the secret, *per omnia saecula*, before the Preface
dialogue. **Palatinate, *Gemaine maß, die kirchen- und gottesdinst anzurichten*, 1546**
(Sehling 14, pp. 96–97):

<!-- doc 472 -->
> Nach der predig soll geschehen ein ermanung an die communicanten aus hertzog Ottheinrichs
> kirchenordnung oder dergleichen vor dem altar und hebt alsdann an zu singen: per omnia secula,
> chorus: amen, der prister: Dominus vobiscum, chorus: et cum spiritu tuo, priester: sursum
> corda, corus: habemus ad Dominum, prister: gratias agamus domino Deo nostro, chorus: dignum et
> iustum est, prister: vere dignum et iustum est etc. Und uf den hohen festen singt der priester
> die gantze prefation, der chor das noch das sanctus bis uf das agnus Dei, darnach hebe der
> prister an zu singen teutsch das vaterunser in gemeinem thon

After the sermon there shall be made an exhortation to the communicants out of Duke
Ottheinrich's church order, or the like, before the altar; and then [the priest] beginneth to
sing: *Per omnia saecula*; the choir: *Amen*; the priest: *Dominus vobiscum*; the choir: *Et cum
spiritu tuo*; the priest: *Sursum corda*; the choir: *Habemus ad Dominum*; the priest: *Gratias
agamus Domino Deo nostro*; the choir: *Dignum et iustum est*; the priest: *Vere dignum et iustum
est*, etc. And on the high feasts the priest singeth the whole Preface, and the choir thereafter
the Sanctus as far as the Agnus Dei; thereafter the priest beginneth to sing in German the Our
Father in the common tone.

The Breslau churches went further still. A report of 1557 says that the Sunday high Mass was
sung by a chaplain "in his habit, as of old", through the gospel and the *Patrem*; then came the
sermon; "the sermon ended, the chaplain singeth the Preface; after the sermon he elevateth the
sacrament"; and the Mass closed with the Sanctus, Agnus Dei and communion chant, the
postcommunion, "and with the *Benedicamus* or *Ite missa est*" (Sehling 3, p. 404).

### 4.6 The Upper German preaching service with communion

**Württemberg, Baden, Strasbourg and their neighbours.** The Württemberg order of 1553 (and its
copies in Baden 1556, Mömpelgard 1560 and the Palatinate of 1556) has no introit, Kyrie, Gloria,
epistle, Preface, Sanctus or Agnus Dei. The service is the sermon service of every Sunday, to
which the communion is added:
1. a German hymn ("Komm heiliger Geist", "Nun bitten wir" or a psalm "agreeable to the season");
2. the sermon, which on communion days includes "a short instruction" on the Supper;
3. the creed sung in German;
4. the exhortation to the communicants, read at the altar;
5. the general prayer, then "the church shall sing the Our Father in German together";
6. the public confession and absolution;
7. the Words of Institution read "with loud, understandable voice";
8. the distribution, with "Gott sei gelobet", "Jesus Christus unser Heiland" or another hymn;
9. a thanksgiving prayer and the blessing, in one of three forms.

(Sehling 16, pp. 252–255.) The opening rubric is quoted in
[`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §3.4. Strasbourg in 1598 keeps the same
shape: the ordinary Sunday service "until the hymn after the sermon"; then the pastor goes to
the altar while the church sings the German *Grates nunc omnes*, and reads the Württemberg
exhortation (Sehling 20/1, pp. 611–612).

### 4.7 The Reformed Supper

**The Palatinate after 1563.** The Palatine order of 1563, and the Reformed orders that copied
it (Bentheim-Tecklenburg 1588, Ysenburg-Birstein 1598), keep nothing of the ordinaries. After
the sermon on the Supper and the Sunday prayer, the minister reads at the table the "Form for
holding the holy Supper":
- the Words of Institution from 1 Corinthians 11, with the self-examination and the list of
  those excluded;
- the doctrine of the Supper; a prayer ending in the Lord's Prayer;
- the Apostles' Creed, "with mouth and heart";
- a lifting up of hearts "into heaven, where Christ Jesus is", in place of the *Sursum corda*;
- the breaking of the bread with the words of 1 Corinthians 10:16;
- singing, or the reading of John 14–18 or Isaiah 53, during the distribution;
- a thanksgiving from Psalm 103 and a closing prayer. Bentheim-Tecklenburg adds: "After all this
  the psalm is sung, and thereafter the blessing of the Lord spoken" (Sehling 22, p. 275).

The breaking replaced the distribution formula. **Palatinate, *Kirchenordnung*, 1563**
(Sehling 14, p. 386):

<!-- doc 504 -->
> Hie soll der kirchendiener einem jeden vom brodt des herrn brechen und im darreichen sprechen:
> Das brodt, das wir brechen, ist die gemeinschaft des leibs Christi. Und der ander
> kirchendiener im darreichen deß kelchs sprechen: Der kelch der dancksagung, damit wir
> dancksagen, ist die gemeinschaft des bluts Christi

Here shall the minister of the church break of the bread of the Lord to every one, and in giving
it say: The bread which we break is the communion of the body of Christ. And the other minister
of the church, in giving the cup, [shall] say: The cup of thanksgiving, wherewith we give
thanks, is the communion of the blood of Christ.

The Bentheim-Tecklenburg order of 1588 has the same form and the same words
(Sehling 22, pp. 271–275).

### 4.8 The early German Masses

**Before the territorial orders.** The German Masses of 1522–1526 (Kantz at Nördlingen, Müntzer
at Allstedt, the Strasbourg and Worms Masses, Volprecht and Döber at Nürnberg, the Low German
Masses of Bremen, Lippe and Kiel) are compared part by part in
[`CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md`](CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md), §§2–11. They keep the Roman shape and
translate it, the canon included, before the *Deutsche Messe* set the pattern for the parishes.
Two of their ordinaries recur below: Müntzer's fourfold Kyrie (§6.1) and Volprecht's
*Benedicamus* "and never *Ite missa est*" (§13).

  gospel (or chapters in course), a sermon, the Words of Institution, a distribution with
  singing, a thanksgiving and a blessing are in every row.
- **The ordinaries divide the families.** The Saxon, Bugenhagen, Franconian, Prussian and
  Latin-frame orders keep Kyrie, Gloria and Creed, and some form of Sanctus and Agnus Dei. The
  Upper German and Reformed orders keep only a creed: the German "Wir glauben" in Württemberg,
  the Apostles' Creed in the Palatinate.
- **The Preface was the first casualty.** Only the Prussian, Franconian-Hof, Latin-frame and
  Transylvanian orders require it. The Saxon and Bugenhagen orders make it optional or festal;
  the *Deutsche Messe*, Albertine Saxony 1580, Nürnberg 1533 and Württemberg drop it (§10).
- **The Verba and the Lord's Prayer travel.** The Lord's Prayer comes before the Verba (Saxony,

  Brandenburg 1540), as a paraphrase in the exhortation (*Deutsche Messe*, Saxony 1539, 1580),

  (Palatinate) (§11).
- **The sermon has two homes.** It follows the gospel and creed almost everywhere. The *Formula
  missae* allowed it before the introit, and Transylvania (1547) held the first sermon after
  Matins, before the Mass began (Sehling 24, p. 244).

## 6. The Kyrie

The hymns sung as or with the Kyrie, the troped German Kyries and the Kyrie in three tongues are
treated in [`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §6. This section asks how many
acclamations were sung, and which of the old Kyrie chants survived.

### 6.1 Three, four or nine

**The medieval norm was nine.** The Roman Kyrie had nine acclamations: Kyrie three times,
Christe three times, Kyrie three times. The Hannover order of 1536 repeats the common
attribution: "To sing the Kyrie eleison nine times, Gregory the Great set up in the Roman
church, as Platina writeth; yet it came first from the Greek church" (Sehling 6/2, p. 1005).

**Luther: three, not nine.** The *Deutsche Messe* cut the Kyrie to three acclamations, "three
times and not nine times" (§3.3). The threefold Kyrie became the norm of the German parish
service:
- Duke Henry's Saxony (1539): "on ordinary Sundays and in the villages one may sing it plain …
  only three times" (Sehling 1, p. 274);
- the visitation articles for Gnandstein (1539) and the *Gemeine Artikel* for the see of Wurzen
  (1542): "Kyrie eleison three times" (Sehling 1, p. 564; 2, p. 98);
- Prussia (1525, 1544 and 1568): "since it is sung three times", in three tongues (1525); "the
  Kyrie eleison only three times, according to the former order" (1544, 1568)
  (Sehling 4, pp. 32, 64, 81);
- the Palatinate (1546): "the Kyrie eleison, each only once" (Sehling 14, p. 96);
- the Henneberg parishes of Suhl (1562) and Obermaßfeld (1563): "the German Kyrie three times"
  (Sehling 2, pp. 352, 346);
- Waldeck (1556): "a Kyrie eleison three times" (Sehling 9, p. 247).

**Nine on the feasts.** The ninefold Kyrie survived where there was a choir, and especially on
feasts. At Wittenberg the plain threefold Kyrie was "the right Kyrie", the ninefold the festal
one (Sehling 1, p. 704; quoted in §7.1). The Pomeranian order of 1542 gives the Sunday Kyrie
"three or nine times, or the Kyrie of the feast" (Sehling 4, p. 358). The
Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel order of 1543 gives the reason for three, and leaves the festal number
to the schoolmaster. **Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel, *Christlike kerken-ordeninge*, 1543**
(Sehling 6/1, p. 54):

<!-- doc 1972 -->
> Id is nicht van nöden, negen mal dat eleison edder Kyrie tho repetirende, yd is genoch dremal
> im namen des Vaders und des Sons und des hilgen Geistes tho singende ane orgelen. Doch mögen
> de scholere in festis wol ein ander Kyrie singen, besundern, wenn se in figurativis singen,
> dremal, veermal edder ock negenmal na gevallen des scholemeisters, also doch, dat man yd mit
> dem singen und orgelen nicht tho lange make, dat de predike darmede nicht vorhindert werde.

It is not needful to repeat the *eleison* or Kyrie nine times; it is enough to sing it three
times, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost, without the organ. Yet
the scholars may well sing another Kyrie on the feasts, especially when they sing in figured
music, three times, four times or nine times, at the schoolmaster's pleasure; yet so that one
make it not too long with the singing and the organ, that the sermon be not hindered thereby.

**Four.** A fourfold form, Kyrie, Christe, Kyrie, Kyrie, appears in three places:
- Müntzer's *Deutsch evangelisch Messe* of 1524: "Kyrie leyson, Christe leyson, kyrie leyson,
  kyrie leyson" (Sehling 1, p. 499);
- the German Masses of the Calenberg-Göttingen order of 1542, which take Müntzer's chants: some
  have the fourfold form, others the threefold
  (Sehling 6/2, pp. 812, 817, 825, 827, 829; three: pp. 820, 823, 832);
- the song appendix of the Hessian *Agende* of 1574, after the German introit for Trinity:
  "Kyrie eleison. Christe eleison. Kyrie eleison. Kyrie eleison" (Sehling 8, p. 465).

The fourth acclamation is the closing Kyrie of the chant, sung to its own melody, kept when the
middle repetitions were cut. Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel's "four times" in figured music (above)
is the same shape set polyphonically.

**One, by three choirs.** At Hof (1592) the German Kyrie was divided among three choirs, one
acclamation each: "the first choir singeth Kyrie eleison in German, the second Christe eleison,
the third, or the girls' choir, Kyrie eleison". In figured music the organ played each of the
three before the choir answered; otherwise the organ "preludeth once only before the *Et in
terra*" (Sehling 11, p. 425).

**Not found.** No order prescribes a sevenfold Kyrie, and none a sixfold one. The counts in the
corpus are three, four and nine, with the "each once" of the Palatinate (1546) amounting to
three.

### 6.2 Which Kyrie: the seasonal chants

**The old melodies kept.** The *Formula missae* kept the Kyrie "with various melodies for the
various seasons" (§3.2). The northern and Franconian orders name the chants they meant, by their
medieval titles. **Pomerania, *Kirchenordnung*, 1542** (Sehling 4, p. 356):

<!-- doc 1859 -->
> Darup dat kyrie dominicale, efft me mach ock up die sondage, kyrie angelicum, de martiribus,
> effte confessoribus, efft virginibus, ' wo man sie genomet hefft singen, dominicale im advent
> und in der vasten, dat paschale tuschen pingsten und paschen, dat summum up die hohern fest,
> effte dat minus summum.

Thereupon the *Kyrie dominicale*; or one may also sing on the Sundays the *Kyrie angelicum*, *de
martyribus*, or *confessoribus*, or *virginibus*, as they have been named; the *dominicale* in
Advent and in Lent, the *paschale* between Easter and Whitsun, the *summum* on the higher
feasts, or the *minus summum*.

The fullest scheme is the Hof order of 1592, for the boys' daily Mass-shaped service sung from
the "great missal". **Hof, *Ordo ecclesiasticus*, 1592** (Sehling 11, p. 472):

<!-- doc 294 -->
> Mox II. ex missali magno canitur Kyrie certi temporis vel angelicum in Adventu Domini vel
> Kyrie Magnae Deus potentiae de Nativitate Christi, cui Et in terra angelicum subiicitur, vel
> Kyrie dominicale maius post dominicam Septuagesimae usque ad Pascha vel Kyrie paschale vel
> Kyrie Fons bonitatis cum Et in terra angelico vel Kyrie dominicale minus post festum
> Trinitatis vel de apostolis, martyribus etc., prout tempus requirit et libri ordo monstrat.

Next, secondly, there is sung out of the great missal the Kyrie of the proper season: either the
*angelicum* in the Advent of the Lord, or the Kyrie *Magne Deus potentiae* of the Nativity of
Christ, to which the angelic *Et in terra* is joined; or the greater *dominicale* from the
Sunday Septuagesima until Easter; or the *paschale*; or the Kyrie *Fons bonitatis* with the
angelic *Et in terra*; or the lesser *dominicale* after the feast of Trinity; or that of the
apostles, martyrs, etc., as the season requireth and the order of the book showeth.

The same order assigns, Sunday by Sunday, a "Kyrie angelicum seu feriale" in Advent, a "Kyrie
paschale" from Easter to the Ascension, and a "Kyrie dominicale" from the fourth Sunday after
Trinity to Advent, with the Sanctus and Agnus Dei of the same set (Sehling 11, pp. 432–450).

**Other lists.**
- **Buxtehude 1526**: a table of the Kyries by occasion: "*In summis festivitatibus et dominica
  Trinitatis* … *Minus summum*", "*In festis beatae virginis Mariae*", "*Kyrie paschale*",
  "*Dominicale*", "*In Adventu Domini*", "*Angelicum*", "*Kyrie apostolicum*"
  (Sehling 7/1, p. 123).
- **Nördlingen 1544**: German Kyries "according to the season, of which we have three at this
  time": the *paschale* from Easter to Whitsun, *Fons bonitatis* from Whitsun to Christmas,
  *Magne Deus* from Christmas to Easter; and the "*Kyrie summum* with the *Et in terra*" at
  Whitsun and Trinity, with the "*Sanctus summus*" (Sehling 12, pp. 318, 321–322).
- **Mecklenburg, *Ordeninge der misse*, 1540/1545**: the Kyrie "according to the season, as hath
  been kept hitherto"; in the villages the pastors are to teach the people "the German *Kyrie
  summum*, *Ach vader, alder högeste godt*" (Sehling 5, p. 151).
- **Schleswig-Holstein 1542**: "The Kyrie eleison may be sung with manifold notes according to
  the difference of the season, as hath been kept hitherto" (Sehling 23, p. 90).
- **Pfalz-Zweibrücken 1557**: the song index lists a *Kyrie paschale*, a Kyrie "*in adventu
  domini*", an *angelicum* and an *apostolicum*, each with its Gloria (Sehling 18, p. 255). The
  hymn plan of 1565 marks "the second Kyrie" for ordinary Sundays (Sehling 18, p. 337).
- **Schweinfurt 1576**: the organ begins "the Latin *Kyrie dominicale*, which the choir
  repeateth" (Sehling 11, pp. 647–648).

So the old cycle of Kyrie chants, *Lux et origo* (*paschale*), *Fons bonitatis*, *Magne Deus*,
*Cunctipotens* (*apostolicum*), *Orbis factor* (*dominicale*) and the rest, survived in the
Lutheran churches that had Latin schools, assigned to the seasons as before. Where the orders
print a text, it is the plain Kyrie, or a new German trope set to the old melody; the old Latin
tropes are not printed ([`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §6.4).

### 6.3 Which language

**Latin, German, Greek.** The Kyrie is the one ordinary the orders most often leave
untranslated. The Homberg synod of 1526 kept "these accustomed little words, *Kyrie eleyson*,
*Hallelujah*, *Hosiannah*, *Sabaoth*, *Amen*", to be explained to the people (Sehling 8, p. 45).
Bugenhagen defended the Greek at length (Braunschweig 1528, Sehling 6/1, p. 438;
[`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §6.3). Prussia sang it in three languages,
"since it is sung three times". **Prussia, *Artikel der ceremonien und anderer kirchen ordnung*,
1525** (Sehling 4, p. 32):

<!-- doc 1832 -->
> Von dem kyrieleyson ist fur gut angesehen, dieweil es dreimal gesungen wirt, das es in dreien
> zungen, wie man auch alhier pfleget, krichisch, lateinisch und deütsch gefangen werde. Gloria
> in excelsis, und et in terra, mögen deutsch oder lateinisch nach bequemigkeit gesungen werden.

Of the Kyrie eleison it is thought good, since it is sung three times, that it be sung in three
tongues, as is the custom here also: Greek, Latin and German. *Gloria in excelsis* and *Et in
terra* may be sung in German or Latin, as is convenient.

The Livonian towns took the same rule in 1533 (Sehling 5, p. 15).

## 7. The Gloria

The German Gloria hymns ("Allein Gott in der Höh", "All Ehr und Lob"), the alternation of Latin
and German Sunday by Sunday, and the omission of the Gloria in Advent, Lent and winter are
treated in [`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §7. Three further points belong
to the ordo.

### 7.1 The Gloria belongs to the festal Kyrie

**Not after the plain Kyrie.** In the medieval Mass the Gloria went with a festal Kyrie and was
silent on ferias and penitential days. Wittenberg kept the rule. **Wittenberg, *Kirchen-Ordnung
für die Stadt Wittenberg*, 1533** (Sehling 1, p. 704):

<!-- doc 148 -->
> Darnach das rechte kyrie dreimal, oder zu zeiten, besondern uf die feste, ein anders neunmal,
> wie gewonlich. Auf das schlechte kyrie singet man nicht gloria in excelsis deo, sondern auf
> andere, wenn man will, und sonderlich uf die feste.

Thereafter the right Kyrie three times; or at times, especially on the feasts, another one nine
times, as is customary. After the plain Kyrie one singeth not the *Gloria in excelsis Deo*, but
after the others, when one will, and especially on the feasts.

The Brandenburg order of 1540 ties the two together: the Kyrie "according to occasion", then the
Gloria, "which together with the *Et in terra* belongeth to the Kyrie" (Sehling 3, p. 68). The
*Formula missae* left it to the bishop "how often he will have it left out" (§3.2), and the
*Deutsche Messe* left it out altogether.

### 7.2 Intonation, *Et in terra*, and the divided Gloria

**The priest intones.** Where the Gloria was sung, the priest (or a deacon, or at Hof a boy)
intoned *Gloria in excelsis Deo*, and the choir continued *Et in terra pax*. In the German
orders the priest sang "Ehre sei Gott in der Höhe" and the people answered with "Allein Gott".
The orders of the north divided the Latin Gloria between the scholars, the people and the organ.
**Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel, *Christlike kerken-ordeninge*, 1543** (Sehling 6/1, p. 55):

<!-- doc 1972 -->
> Na dem Kyrie singet de prester: Gloria in excelsis Deo. Dat scholerchor antwerdet und singet:
> Et in terra pax, hominibus bona voluntas, nicht mehr. Balde singet de ganze kercke: Alleine
> Got in der höged sy ehr etc. vul uth.

After the Kyrie the priest singeth: *Gloria in excelsis Deo*. The scholars' choir answereth and
singeth: *Et in terra pax hominibus bonae voluntatis*, no more. Straightway the whole church
singeth "To God alone on high be glory," etc., in full.

The scholars and the organ then continue the Latin *Laudamus te* "in full" (Sehling 6/1, p. 55).
So the Gloria was sung twice over: once in its Latin form, divided between the priest, the
scholars and the organ, and once as Decius's hymn by the people. The Pomeranian order of 1542
allows either half to be dropped "when it will grow too long" (Sehling 4, p. 356).

**At Hof**, the minister intoned a Gloria "suited to the Mass and answering to the season"; the
organ began the *Et in terra*, "divided into certain portions", which the three choirs finished
in alternation with the organ. In the hardest frost the organ only began it and the choir sang
the rest (Sehling 11, p. 425).

### 7.3 The Gloria and the Kyrie as one piece

**One unit.** Because the Gloria followed the Kyrie and shared its chant, several orders treat
the two as a single piece: "the Kyrie and the *Et in terra*" (Brandenburg-Nürnberg 1533;
Kurpfalz 1546; Transylvania 1547; Nördlingen 1544), "Kyrie eleison with the *Gloria in excelsis
et in terra pax*, as it is put into German and printed in the song-books" (Hesse 1566, Sehling
8, p. 239). The Hof order of 1592 assigns to each seasonal Kyrie its own *Et in terra*: "the
Kyrie *Magne Deus potentiae* … to which the angelic *Et in terra* is joined" (§6.2). In East
Frisia (1535) the Kyrie and Gloria together were sung "as it is sung in the song *Allein Gott in
der Höh sei Ehr*" ([`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §6.5).

## 8. Salutation, collect and Pax

The collect and postcommunion are treated in [`COLLECTS_GUIDE.md`](COLLECTS_GUIDE.md). Here the
fixed formulas around them.

**The salutation.** *Dominus vobiscum* before the collect survived in most of the Latin-shaped
orders, often given in German beside the Latin:
- Mecklenburg 1552: "*Dominus vobiscum*, or *Der herr sei mit euch*. Chorus: *Et cum spiritu
  tuo*, or *Und mit deinem geiste*" (Sehling 5, p. 198);
- Brandenburg-Nürnberg 1533: "*Dominus vobiscum* or *Der Herr sei mit euch*"
  (Sehling 11, p. 188);
- Hof 1592: "*Der Herr sei mit euch!* Chorus: *Und mit deinem geist*", sung "either chorally or
  figurally" (Sehling 11, p. 425);
- Transylvania 1547: before the Preface and again before the postcommunion collect
  (Sehling 24, p. 244).

Bugenhagen's Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel order dropped it: "*Dominus vobiscum*, or *De Here sy mit
ju*, is not needful to say" (Sehling 6/1, p. 55). The *Deutsche Messe* and the Württemberg and
Reformed orders have no salutation.

**The Pax.** The *Formula missae* kept *Pax Domini sit semper vobiscum* after the Lord's Prayer
as "a kind of public absolution from sins … the one and most worthy preparation for the Lord's
Table" (Sehling 1, p. 6). The Pax stayed where the Lord's Prayer stayed near the altar:
- Prussia 1525: "the priest turneth to the people and giveth them the evangelical absolution, or
  the peace, and singeth in German *Pax domini*" (Sehling 4, p. 32);
- Brandenburg-Nürnberg 1533, after the Lord's Prayer: "*Pax Domini sit semper vobiscum*, or else
  in German" (§4.3);
- Brandenburg 1540: "*Der fried des herren sei mit euch allen*. Let all the people say *Amen*"
  (Sehling 3, p. 69);
- the Palatinate 1546, after the Verba: "*der fride deß herrn sei mit euch allen*. The choir
  answereth: *Amen*" (Sehling 14, p. 97);
- Hof 1592: "*Der frid des Herren sei mit euch allen*" (Sehling 11, p. 426).

The *Deutsche Messe*, the Saxon orders of 1539 and 1580, the Pomeranian and Mecklenburg orders
and Württemberg have no Pax.

**The Preface dialogue** is treated with the Preface (§10).

## 9. The Creed

The German creed-hymn "Wir glauben all" and its places in the service are treated in
[`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §9. This section asks which creed was used,
and who began it.

### 9.1 Intoned by the priest, sung by the choir, then by the people

**Three layers.** In the Saxon and Bugenhagen orders the creed was sung three times over: the
priest intoned the Nicene Creed in Latin at the altar, the scholars sang the Latin *Patrem* to
the end, and the people then sang "Wir glauben all". **Wittenberg, *Kirchen-Ordnung für die
Stadt Wittenberg*, 1533** (Sehling 1, p. 704):

<!-- doc 148 -->
> Darnach singt der priester das evangelium zum volk gewandt, darnach widerumb zum altar credo
> in unum deum. Darauf singen die schuler patrem lateinisch, alsdan mit dem volk wir gleuben
> alle an einen gott. Oft aber liebt der schulmeister bald auf das evangelium an wir gleuben
> etc. um der kurz willen.

Thereafter the priest singeth the gospel turned toward the people, thereafter, [turned] again
toward the altar, *Credo in unum Deum*. Thereupon the scholars sing the *Patrem* in Latin, then
with the people "We all believe in one God". But often the schoolmaster beginneth "We believe",
etc., straight after the gospel, for brevity's sake.

The same three layers are in Pomerania 1535 ("the *Patrem* in full, *dominicaliter*"),
Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel 1543, Duke Henry's Saxony 1539 ("the Latin *Patrem* in full, after the
common notes"), Mecklenburg 1552 (with the Latin text and notes), the Palatinate 1546 and Saxony
1580 (Sehling 4, p. 341; 6/1, p. 55; 1, p. 274; 5, p. 198; 14, p. 96; 1, p. 368). Brandenburg
(1540) sang the Latin *Patrem* "in cathedrals and foundations", and "Wir glauben" in the
parishes (Sehling 3, p. 68).

**Not intoned.** Prussia (1525) abolished the priest's intonation: "The *Credo* or creed the
priest shall not begin as heretofore, but it shall be sung in German by the choir and the
people" (Sehling 4, p. 32).

### 9.2 Nicene, Apostles' or Athanasian

**The Nicene Creed in the Lutheran Mass.** The Latin *Credo* of the Mass is the Nicene Creed,
and "Wir glauben all" is Luther's paraphrase of it. Sangerhausen (1555) calls the people's
German creed "the customary *Symbolum Nicaenum* in German" (Sehling 1, p. 658). Weissenfels
(1578) sometimes sang "the *Symbolum Nicenum*" in figured music before the German creed
(Sehling 1, p. 693).

**The Apostles' Creed in Hesse and the Reformed orders.** The Hessian order of 1566 put the
Apostles' Creed in the Mass, sung by everyone every Sunday, and kept the Nicene and Athanasian
Creeds for the feasts of Christ. **Hesse, *Kirchenordnung*, 1566** (Sehling 8, pp. 242–243):

<!-- doc 2257 -->
> Es wird aber dies symbolum und bekentnus des glaubens von der ganzen kirchen allen sontag mit
> heller stim nach der lection des evangelii gesungen und dasselbige aus volgenden ursachen […]
> Auf die feirtage aber Christi mag man singen oder lesen das symbolum Nicenum oder Athanasii,
> welche dann sehr nütz sein zur sterkung unsers glaubens und zu widerstehen den ketzern, so uf
> mancherlei weis die ehr und die zwo naturen Christi, des Sohns Gottes angefochten und
> gelestert haben

But this creed and confession of faith is sung by the whole church every Sunday with clear voice
after the reading of the gospel, and that for the following causes […] But on the feasts of
Christ one may sing or read the Nicene Creed or that of Athanasius, which are very profitable
for the strengthening of our faith and for withstanding the heretics who in manifold ways have
assailed and blasphemed the honour and the two natures of Christ, the Son of God.

The Palatine order of 1563 has the communicants confess the Apostles' Creed "with mouth and
heart" inside the Supper form (§4.7). Nördlingen (1579) had "an article of the Apostles' Creed
with the short exposition of Luther's catechism" read before the German creed on feast days
(Sehling 12, p. 366).

**The Athanasian Creed** was read or sung on Trinity Sunday in some places: as the text of the
early sermon at Schweinfurt (1543) and at Altenburg (1554) (Sehling 11, p. 638; 1, p. 519). Its
place was otherwise the morning office ([`OFFICES_GUIDE.md`](OFFICES_GUIDE.md), §7).

### 9.3 Where the creed fell

After the gospel nearly everywhere; after the sermon in Württemberg, where the creed was sung
"in German" when the sermon was over (Sehling 16, p. 252); and inside the Supper form in the
Palatinate. The places of the creed-hymn as offertory, as cover for the preacher's movement to
the pulpit, and as a substitute for "Nun bitten wir" are in
[`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §§9.1–9.4.

### 9.4 Which creed, order by order

**Four practices.** A search of the corpus for the creeds named in or near the Mass (*Credo in
unum Deum*, *Patrem*, *symbolum Nicenum*, *symbolum apostolicum*, *Credo in Deum Patrem*, "Ich
glaub in Gott Vater", *Athanasii*) gives four practices:
- **The Nicene Creed only**, in Latin, in German verse by verse, or as Luther's "Wir glauben
  all". This is the rule of the Saxon, Bugenhagen and Franconian orders.
- **The Apostles' Creed only**, sung or said: Strasbourg, the foundation Mass at Goslar, the
  Upper German pulpit, the Reformed Supper.
- **A choice left open**, Nicene or Apostles', Latin or German, with no rule for when.
- **A choice by occasion**: one creed on Sundays, another on feasts.

**Strasbourg: the Apostles' Creed sung, the Nicene by some.** The Strasbourg German Mass of 1524
prints the Apostles' Creed with notes after the sermon, in Matthäus Greiter's setting, and
leaves the Nicene to those who want it. **Strasbourg, *Ordenung und inhalt Teutscher Mess*,
1524** (Sehling 20/1, p. 138):

<!-- doc 1279 -->
> Folget die Predig Darnach der Glaub Ich glaub in Got Vater, den almechtigen, schöpffer himmels
> und der erden […] Das groß Patrem, das man nennt Symbolum Nicenum, würt von etlichen gesungen

Then followeth the sermon. Thereafter the Creed: "I believe in God the Father Almighty, maker of
heaven and earth" […] The great *Patrem*, which is called the Nicene Creed, is sung by some.

The earlier Strasbourg Mass of Theobald Schwarz (1524) had the Nicene Creed in German, "Ich
glaub in ein Gott, den almechtigen vattern" (Sehling 20/1, p. 121).

**By occasion: Sundays and feasts.** Hesse (1566) gave the Apostles' Creed to every Sunday and
the Nicene or Athanasian to the feasts of Christ (§9.2). The Rothenberg ministers of 1618
reversed the scheme: "Wir glauben" on Sundays, the Apostles' Creed word for word on the other
feasts. **Rothenberg, *Christliche vereinigung* of the ministers, 1618** (Sehling 13, p. 548):

<!-- doc 460 -->
> Nach abtretung der knaben, sing man die sontäg den gewöhnlichen glauben: Wir glauben all an
> einen Gott etc. Die andern feiertäg aber das symbolum apostolicum deutsch von worts zu worts:
> Ich glaub in Gott Vater, allmechtigen etc.

After the boys have stepped down, let the customary creed be sung on the Sundays: "We all
believe in one God", etc. But on the other holy days the Apostles' Creed in German, word for
word: "I believe in God the Father Almighty", etc.

**A free choice of three.** The Hessian *Agende* of 1574 lets the Apostles' Creed, Luther's
creed-hymn or the Nicene be sung after the gospel. On occasion the Nicene or the Athanasian
Creed might instead be read aloud at the altar. **Hesse, *Agende*, 1574** (Sehling 8, p. 411):

<!-- doc 2272 -->
> Auf verlesung des evangelii wird gesungen das symbolum apostolicum teutsch, von wort zu wort,
> oder wie es Doctor Luther paraphrastice in gesangsweise gestelt hat, oder das symbolum Nicenum
> teutsch. Man mag auch je bisweilen nach dem evangelio das teutsch Grates nunc omnes, oder
> einen andern kurzen gesang singen und darauf das symbolum Nicenum oder Athanasianum mit klarer
> stimm dem volk für dem altar fürlesen

Upon the reading of the gospel is sung the Apostles' Creed in German, word for word, or as
Doctor Luther hath set it in the manner of a song by way of paraphrase, or the Nicene Creed in
German. One may also now and then, after the gospel, sing the German *Grates nunc omnes*, or
another short song, and thereupon read the Nicene or Athanasian Creed with a clear voice to the
people before the altar.

The Hessian *Agende* counts "Wir glauben all" as a paraphrase of the Apostles' Creed, not of the
Nicene. The Corvey order for Bruchhausen (1603) does the same: "the Apostles' Creed, as Dr
Luther set it in German in the manner of a song", where Sehling's editors identify the song as
"Wir glauben all" (Sehling 21, p. 244). Most orders, and the modern editors, take it as the

| Hesse 1566 | 8, pp. 242–243, 254 | Apostles' Creed every Sunday; "Wir glauben" or the Apostles' Creed in the shorter service | Nicene or Athanasian on the feasts of Christ |
| Prussia 1568 | 4, p. 81 | Latin Nicene in the cathedral | elsewhere alternating Sunday by Sunday with "Wir glauben" |
| Pomerania, *Agenda*, 1569 | 4, p. 438 | Nicene in Latin or Low German, or "Wi gelöven" | — |
| Kurland 1570 | 5, p. 88 | Latin *Patrem*, or the Apostles' Creed sung ("Ich gleub an gott vater"), or "Wir gleuben" | — |
| Hesse, *Agende*, 1574 | 8, p. 411 | Apostles' word for word, Luther's paraphrase, or Nicene in German | Nicene or Athanasian sometimes read aloud |
| Weissenfels 1578 | 1, p. 693 | figured Nicene before the German creed | some days |
| Corvey (Bruchhausen) 1603 | 21, p. 244 | "Wir glauben all", called the Apostles' Creed | — |
| Rothenberg 1618 | 13, p. 548 | "Wir glauben all" on Sundays | Apostles' word for word on other feasts |

**What the table shows.**
- The Nicene Creed, sung in Latin by the choir and in German by the people, is the Lutheran norm
  north and east.
- The Apostles' Creed entered the Mass from three directions:
  - the Upper German sermon service (Strasbourg, Schwäbisch Hall, Württemberg's pulpit);
  - the Reformed Supper (Frankfurt's French church, the Palatinate);
  - Hesse, where it was the creed of the whole congregation every Sunday.
- The Athanasian Creed was never the Sunday creed. It was read on the feasts of Christ in Hesse
  and on Trinity Sunday in a few places (§9.2), and otherwise belonged to the morning office.
- The weekday offices of Naumburg (1537) and Nördlingen (1544, 1579) gave the Nicene Creed its
  own day, Friday or Thursday, in a weekly round of canticles
  (Sehling 2, p. 72; 12, pp. 326, 377); that is office, not Mass.

## 10. The Preface and the Sanctus

The Proper Prefaces, their texts and the days they were used, are in
[`PROPERS_GUIDE.md`](PROPERS_GUIDE.md), §8 and Appendix A; the German Sanctus hymns ("Jesaia dem
Propheten", the Trinitarian "Heilig ist Gott der Vater") in
[`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §12.

### 10.1 The Preface: required, optional, festal or gone

**Four answers.** The orders give four answers to the question whether the Preface was sung:
- **Required.** The *Formula missae*, Prussia 1525, Brandenburg 1540, Transylvania 1547 and the
  Breslau Mass of 1557 have it in the ordo, with the dialogue (§§4.4–4.5).
- **If time allows.** Mecklenburg (1552) makes it the priest's choice "if the time permit".
- **Festal.** Wittenberg 1533 (feasts, and Trinity on Sundays "if one will"), Duke Henry's
  Saxony 1539 ("on the feasts and now and then on Sundays"), Pomerania 1535 ("not always …
  especially at the high feasts"), the Palatinate 1546 (the whole Preface "on the high feasts",
  the dialogue always) and the boys' daily service at Hof (from Lossius's book "on the chief
  festivals") (Sehling 1, pp. 704, 274; 4, p. 341; 14, p. 97; 11, p. 473).
- **Gone.** The *Deutsche Messe*, Nürnberg 1533, Albertine Saxony 1580, Württemberg and the
  Reformed orders. The Palatinate's Reformed form keeps only the idea of the *Sursum corda*, as
  an exhortation to lift up the heart "into heaven, where Christ Jesus is" (§4.7).

The Mecklenburg rubric shows the whole Preface section made optional. **Mecklenburg,
*Kirchenordnung*, 1552** (Sehling 5, p. 199):

<!-- doc 1922 -->
> So es denn die zeit leidet, mag der priester die prefation singen, deudsch oder latinisch,
> anzufahen mit diesen worten: Dominus vobiscum, oder Der herr sei mit euch. Und findest die
> prefationes auch hernach an irem ort bei sammen mit den gewönlichen noten verzeichnet. Darauf
> wird das sanctus latinisch gesungen, wie es auch nach der teglichen prefation gesatzt, oder
> deudsch Esaia dem propheten das geschach, wie es im gesangbüchlin stehet. So man zeit hat, mag
> der priester auch eine vermanung und unterricht vom hochwirdigen sacrament dem volk furlesen.
> Wie auch nach den prefationibus verzeichnet. Nach der vermanung, oder wenn um kurz der zeit
> willen, die vermanung und prefation unterlassen werden, alsbald nach der predigt singe der
> priester

If then the time permit, the priest may sing the Preface, in German or in Latin, beginning with
these words: *Dominus vobiscum*, or "The Lord be with you". And thou findest the Prefaces also
set down together hereafter in their place with the customary notes. Thereupon the Sanctus is
sung in Latin, as it is also set after the daily Preface, or in German, "Isaiah the prophet, it
befell", as it standeth in the song-book. If there be time, the priest may also read to the
people an exhortation and instruction concerning the most worthy sacrament, as is also set down
after the Prefaces. After the exhortation, or, when for shortness of time the exhortation and
Preface are left out, straightway after the sermon, let the priest sing [the Our Father and the
Words of the Testament].

### 10.2 Where the Sanctus fell

**Five places.** The Sanctus is the ordinary that moved most:
1. **After the Preface, before the Verba** (the Roman place): Mecklenburg 1552, Pomerania 1535,
   Brandenburg 1540, the Palatinate 1546, Transylvania 1547.
2. **After the Verba**, which were sung in the Preface: the *Formula missae* ("with the
   elevation during the Benedictus") and Prussia 1525 (§4.4).
3. **After the Verba, without a Preface**: Brandenburg-Nürnberg 1533, followed by the Lord's
   Prayer and the Pax (§4.3).
4. **During the distribution**, in German: the *Deutsche Messe*, Duke Henry's Saxony 1539,
   Albertine Saxony 1580, Mecklenburg 1552 (as an alternative), Hof 1592 (the Latin Sanctus and
   Agnus "of the season" sung during the communion), Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel 1543 ("at another
   time, or at all times, under the communion").
5. **Nowhere**: Württemberg and the Reformed orders.

### 10.3 With or without the Benedictus

**The Latin Sanctus kept its Benedictus.** Where the Latin Sanctus was sung, it was sung whole,
with the *Benedictus qui venit* and its *Hosanna*. The *Formula missae* had the elevation "under
the singing of the *Benedictus*" (Sehling 1, p. 6). The Palatinate order of 1546 has the choir
sing "the Sanctus as far as the Agnus Dei", that is, through the *Benedictus*
(Sehling 14, p. 97), and the Amberg hospital church in 1544 "the Sanctus as far as the Agnus
Dei" before the German Lord's Prayer (Sehling 13, p. 283).

**Luther's German Sanctus dropped it.** "Jesaia dem Propheten" paraphrases Isaiah 6 only, and so
has no *Benedictus* and no *Hosanna*. Where it replaced the Latin Sanctus, the *Benedictus* went
with it.

**The other German Sanctus kept it.** The prose German Sanctus of the early German Masses, and
the Trinitarian Sanctus of Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel, keep the *Benedictus* and the Hebrew
*Hosianna*:
- Kantz (Nördlingen 1522): "Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Sabaoth; full are heaven and earth of thy
  glory. Hosanna in the highest! Blessed be he that cometh in the name of the Lord. Luck and
  salvation in the highest!" (Sehling 12, p. 286), with *Hosanna* rendered *Glück und heil*;
- Bremen 1525, in Low German, the same (Sehling 7/2.2, p. 406);
- Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel 1543: "Holy is God the Father, holy is God the Son, holy is God the
  Holy Ghost … *Hosianna* in the highest. Blessed be he that cometh in the name of the Lord.
  *Hosianna* in the highest" (Sehling 6/1, p. 59);
- Dortmund 1554, Low German, with the gloss "*Hosianna* (ah, make us blessed)"
  (Sehling 21, p. 211);
- the Neuenrade order of 1564 prints two Low German Sanctus texts, both with "*Gelovet sy, de da
  kümt im namen des Heren*" (Sehling 22, p. 532);
- Volprecht and Döber at Nürnberg (1524–1525) rendered *Hosanna* "*Mach uns selig in der höhe*",
  "make us blessed in the highest"
  (Sehling 11, pp. 41, 54; [`CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md`](CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md), §§7–8).

## 11. The Lord's Prayer and the Words of Institution

The Words of Institution, their wording, their chant and the elevation are treated order by
order in [`CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md`](CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md); the people's Lord's Prayer
hymn in [`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §13. This section compares their
order.

**Seven sequences.**

| Sequence | Orders |
|---|---|
| Preface with Verba → Sanctus → Lord's Prayer → Pax → communion | *Formula missae* 1523; Prussia 1525 (with the Agnus before the Pax) |
| Exhortation → Verba → Sanctus → Lord's Prayer → Pax → communion | Brandenburg-Nürnberg 1533 |
| Preface → Sanctus → Verba → Lord's Prayer → Pax → Agnus → exhortation → communion | Brandenburg 1540 |
| (Preface → Sanctus →) Lord's Prayer → Verba → communion | Wittenberg 1533; Pomerania 1535; Mecklenburg 1552; Transylvania 1547; Palatinate 1546 (Verba → Pax); Hof 1592 (Lord's Prayer → Pax → Verba) |
| Paraphrase of the Lord's Prayer + exhortation → Verba → communion | *Deutsche Messe* 1526; Duke Henry's Saxony 1539; Albertine Saxony 1580; Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel 1543 (at times) |
| Lord's Prayer hymn → confession → Verba read → communion | Württemberg 1553 |
| Prayer ending in the Lord's Prayer → creed → breaking of bread | Palatinate 1563; Bentheim-Tecklenburg 1588 |

**The bread and the cup apart.** The *Deutsche Messe* gave the bread after the words over the
bread and the cup after the words over the cup. Albertine Saxony (1580) required this "in the
villages, where this sacrament is given out by one person only" (Sehling 1, p. 369), and Prussia
(1544) followed it (Sehling 4, pp. 65–66). Elsewhere both words were said or sung before the
distribution.

**The elevation.** Kept with the Verba in the *Formula missae*, Prussia 1525, Wittenberg 1533,
Brandenburg 1540 and Breslau 1557; abolished in the Palatinate 1546 and in Mecklenburg 1552,
"that the inequality bring not contention" (Sehling 5, p. 199). See
[`CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md`](CANON_IN_GERMAN_MASSES.md) and
[`LITURGICAL_CALENDAR_GUIDE.md`](LITURGICAL_CALENDAR_GUIDE.md), §10.6.

## 12. The Agnus Dei

The German Agnus hymns ("Christe du Lamm Gottes", "O Lamm Gottes unschuldig") and their place
after the communion are in [`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §§14–15.

### 12.1 How many times

**Three, as of old.** The Latin Agnus Dei three times, the third ending *dona nobis pacem*, is
printed in full in Pfalz-Neuburg 1543 and Brandenburg 1540 (Sehling 13, p. 75; 3, p. 69).
Regensburg had the schoolmaster sing it "three times slowly" during the distribution, and more
songs if the three were not enough (1542 and 1553: Sehling 13, pp. 393, 420). Luther's German
"Christe du Lamm Gottes" kept the threefold form: "*Christe, du lamb gottes … erbarm dich
unser*. The third time thus: *gib uns deinen friden, amen*" (Wittenberg 1533, Sehling 1, p.
705). Hamburg (1529) and Neuenrade (1564) say the same in Low German
(Sehling 5, p. 529; 22, p. 532). Hof sang "O Lamm Gottes unschuldig" three times, "the last time
with these words added: *Gib uns dein fride, o Jesu*" (Sehling 11, p. 429). Müntzer's Allstedt
order had the people sing the Baptist's witness "three times", the third ending "Give us thy
peace" (Sehling 1, p. 506).

**Twice.** The Prussian order of 1525 cut the Agnus Dei to two, "for brevity's sake", so that
the people's hymns during the communion should have more room. **Prussia, *Artikel der
ceremonien und anderer kirchen ordnung*, 1525** (Sehling 4, p. 32):

<!-- doc 1832 -->
> Bald darauf singet der chor das agnus dei, mit wenig noten deütsch oder lateinisch nach
> gelegenheit, doch von kürz wegen allein zweimal, damit die nachfolgenden christlichen gesenge
> der gemeine so vil bas stad haben, und das erste mahel mit dem miserere nobis, zum andern mit
> dem dona nobis pacem zubeschliessen.

Straightway thereupon the choir singeth the Agnus Dei with few notes, in German or Latin as
occasion serveth, yet for brevity's sake only twice, that the Christian songs of the
congregation following may have the more room; and to conclude the first time with the *miserere
nobis*, the second with the *dona nobis pacem*.

The Livonian towns copied it: "the Agnus Dei with few notes, twice … first, *Be gracious to us*;
second, *Give us thy peace*" (Wolmar 1533, Sehling 5, p. 16).

**Once.** The Albertine *Cellische Ordnungen* of 1545 had the choir sing the Latin Agnus Dei
once, after the communion, while the people knelt and gave thanks. **Albertine Saxony, *Die
Cellischen Ordnungen*, 1545** (Sehling 1, p. 301):

<!-- doc 33 -->
> so sol der chor anheben, das lateinische agnus dei nur ein mal singen, in des kniet das volk
> und thut seine danksagung zu got.

then shall the choir begin to sing the Latin Agnus Dei only once; meanwhile the people kneel and
make their thanksgiving to God.

The single German strophe "Christe du Lamm Gottes … erbarm dich unser. Gib uns deinen Frieden",
printed once as the close of the communion, is the form of Duke Henry's Saxony (1539) and the
Calenberg-Göttingen Masses (1542) (Sehling 1, p. 275; 6/2, pp. 819, 824), and Kantz's Mass of
1522 has one strophe combining both endings (Sehling 12, p. 286).

### 12.2 Where it fell

**Before or during the communion.** In the Roman place, before the communion, in Prussia 1525
and Brandenburg 1540; during the distribution in the *Formula missae*, Nürnberg 1533, Pomerania
1535, Mecklenburg 1552, Transylvania 1547, Albertine Saxony 1580 and Hof 1592.

**After the communion.** The German Agnus "Christe du Lamm Gottes" after the communion as its
close in Wittenberg 1533 ("when the communion is ended"), Hamburg 1529 ("when they have all
communicated and are in their places"), Pomerania 1535 and Mecklenburg 1552. Pomerania limits
the Latin Agnus and the hymns during the communion: "but not longer than the communion lasteth"
(Sehling 4, p. 341).

## 13. The dismissal and the blessing

The Roman Mass ended with *Ite missa est* (or *Benedicamus Domino* in Masses without the
Gloria), and then the priest's blessing, *Benedicat vos omnipotens Deus, Pater et Filius et
Spiritus Sanctus*, a Trinitarian blessing (Sehling 11, p. 42, note 21). The evangelical orders
changed both. The *Benedicamus* in the offices, its seasonal melodies and its German forms are
treated in [`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §§16.8–16.10; the closing hymns
in §15 of the same guide.

### 13.1 *Ite missa est*, *Benedicamus*, or neither

**Three practices.** A search of every *Ite missa est* and every *Benedicamus* near the end of
the Mass gives three practices:
- **The *Ite* kept.** Only two places:
  - the Breslau report of 1557: "with the *Benedicamus* or *Ite missa est* the Mass is closed"
    (Sehling 3, p. 404);
  - the Brandenburg-Ansbach visitation of 1536, which kept the old order "from the *Confiteor*
    to the end of the Mass, *Ite missa est*", so far as it was not against Scripture
    (Sehling 11, p. 326). The Ansbach order of 1548 then put "the *Benedicamus* for the *Ite
    missa*" (Sehling 11, p. 327).
- **The *Benedicamus* in its place**, in Latin or in German (lists below).
- **Neither.** Luther's *Deutsche Messe* and most Saxon, Bugenhagen, Upper German and Reformed
  orders end with the thanksgiving collect and the blessing, and at most a hymn after it.
  Pomerania (1535): "after the Amen nothing more is sung" (Sehling 4, p. 341).

**Never *Ite missa est*.** Volprecht's Nürnberg Mass makes the rule explicit. **Nürnberg,
*Deutsche Messe* of the Prior Volprecht, 1524** (Sehling 11, p. 39):

<!-- doc 247 -->
> Benechctio: ista dicitur et nunquam dicitur Ite missa est; loco ejus dicitur Benedicamus
> Domino Responsio: Deo gratias.

The blessing: this is said, and never is *Ite missa est* said; in its place is said *Benedicamus
Domino*; response: *Deo gratias*.

**The Latin *Benedicamus*:**
- Luther, *Formula missae* (1523): in the place of the *Ite*, "with alleluia added (where and
  when it pleaseth)", or with the vesper melodies (§3.2; Sehling 1, p. 6);
- Nürnberg, *Form und Ordnung einer christlichen Messe* (1525): "*Benedicamus Domino*,
  *dominicaliter*", then "Es woll uns Gott genädig sein" (Sehling 11, p. 57);
- Goslar (1528): the collect "with the *Benedicamus*" sung by the boys in the choir after the
  distribution (Sehling 7/2.2, p. 242);
- Brandenburg-Nürnberg (1533), followed by the blessing (quoted below, Sehling 11, p. 198);
- Regensburg (1542): the gospeller sings *Deo dicamus gratias*; Regensburg (1553): the second
  minister sings *Benedicamus Domino*, the choir *Deo dicamus gratias*
  (Sehling 13, pp. 393, 421);
- Pfalz-Neuburg (1543): sung by the priest or the deacons "in the tone in which the Kyrie was
  sung", the choir answering in the same tone *Deo dicamus gratias* (Sehling 13, p. 76);
- Transylvania (1547): *Dominus vobiscum*, collect and *Benedicamus*, all concluded "with the
  blessing of the people in the name of the Holy Trinity" (Sehling 24, pp. 223, 244);
- Hohenlohe (1553): "the collect with *Benedicamus Domino*", then the Aaronic blessing
  (Sehling 15, p. 73); the Öhringen foundation (1556) prints its melody with the Mass
  (Sehling 15, p. 104);
- Suhl in Henneberg (1562): "Collect. *Benedicamus*. The blessing out of Moses"
  (Sehling 2, p. 351);
- Wolfstein (1574): "the thanksgiving, the *Benedicamus*, the choir answereth *Deo gratias*, and
  the blessing" (Sehling 13, p. 577);
- Chemnitz (1578): "the collect which is called the *complenda*, and the *Benedicamus* together
  with the benediction, as is customary" (Sehling 1, p. 542).

**The German *Benedicamus*.** The early German Masses put the versicle into German, and Hof kept
it with its own melody in 1592:
- Müntzer, Allstedt (1524): each German Mass ends with a prayer and the *Benedicamus*, "Last uns
  gesegnen den herren, alleluia. Got sei ewiglich dank" (Sehling 1, pp. 497, 502);
- Erfurt, *Deutsches Kirchenamt* (1525), after Müntzer: "Lasst uns gesegnen den herren. Gott sei
  ewiglich dank", then the Aaronic blessing (Sehling 2, p. 380);
- Lippe (1525), in Low German: "Ladt uns benedyen den hern, Gade sy ewich danck", then the
  Aaronic blessing (Sehling 22, p. 569);
- Volprecht's German Mass (1524), after the blessing: "Last uns den Herrn lob und dank sagen!
  Antwort: Gott sei gedankt" (Sehling 11, p. 42);
- Worms (1524): "Last uns gott gebenedeien", then a Trinitarian blessing (quoted below);
- Strasbourg (1524): "Sagen danck dem herren", then the blessing (Sehling 20/1, pp. 133, 139);
- Hof (1592): the deacon "addeth the German *Benedicamus*": "Last uns gebenedeien den Herren.
  Chorus: Gott sei gedankt", then the Aaronic blessing (Sehling 11, pp. 426–427).

**Before or after the blessing.** In most orders that kept it the *Benedicamus* came before the
blessing, as in the Roman Mass. Brandenburg-Nürnberg 1528 put it after: "for the conclusion the
benediction of Numbers 6 in German, thereafter *Benedicamus Domino* sung" (Sehling 11, p. 137).
So did Volprecht's German Mass, and Nürnberg 1525, where the *Benedicamus* is followed by a
hymn.

### 13.2 The blessing: Aaronic, Trinitarian, or both

**Five practices.** The blessings at the end of the Mass were searched in every form: Numbers 6,
Psalm 67, the Trinitarian formulas ("Gott Vater, Sohn und heiliger Geist", *Benedicat vos …
Pater et Filius*), and 2 Corinthians 13:13. They give five practices:
- **The Aaronic blessing alone.** This is the rule, from the *Formula missae* onward. It is in
  the singular ("Der Herr segne dich") in the *Deutsche Messe* and the Saxon orders, and in the
  plural ("Der Herr gesegen euch") in Nürnberg and the south. Examples:
  - Luther's *Deutsche Messe* (1526);
  - Wittenberg (1533);
  - Pomerania (1535);
  - Mecklenburg (1552);
  - Albertine Saxony (1580);
  - Volprecht (1524) and Döber (1525) at Nürnberg;
  - Lippe and Erfurt (1525);
  - Weißenburg and Brandenburg-Nürnberg (1528);
  - Hohenlohe (1553);
  - Suhl (1562);
  - Hof (1592);
  - the Palatinate's Sunday service (1563, Sehling 14, p. 391).
- **A Trinitarian blessing alone**: the early German Masses of Worms and Strasbourg (1524), and
  Regensburg (1542), all quoted below. Transylvania (1547) blesses "in the name of the Holy
  Trinity" without printing the words (Sehling 24, p. 223).
- **The Aaronic and the Trinitarian together**, the second following the first: Strasbourg's
  *Ordenung und inhalt Teutscher Mess* (1524): "Gesegne euch der herr … Der segen Got, des
  vatters und des suns etc." (Sehling 20/1, p. 140); and the Strasbourg Supper of 1598 (quoted
  below).
- **A choice of forms**: the Brandenburg-Nürnberg set of four, and its descendants (below).
- **Different blessings for different occasions**: Prussia (1525) and Strasbourg (1598) (below).

**The Nürnberg set of four.** Brandenburg-Nürnberg gave four blessings to choose from: Numbers
6; a paraphrase of Psalm 67; and two Trinitarian forms. **Brandenburg-Nürnberg,
*Kirchenordnung*, 1533** (Sehling 11, p. 198):

<!-- doc 270 -->
> Benedicamus Domino. Deo gracias. Darnach segne er das volk also: Der Herr gesegen euch und
> behüte euch! Der Herr erleuchte sein angesicht über euch und sei euch gnedig! Der Herr erhebe
> sein angesicht auf euch und gebe euch fride! Amen. Oder also: Gott sei uns gnedig und
> barmherzig und gebe uns seinen götlichen segen! Er laß uns sein angesicht leuchten und gebe
> uns seinen frid! Amen. Oder also: Gesegen und behüte uns, Gott der Vater und der Sune und der
> Heilig Gaist! Amen. Oder also: Der segen Gott des Vaters und des Suns und des Heiligen Gaists
> sei mit euch und bleib allezeit mit uns allen! Amen.

*Benedicamus Domino. Deo gratias.* Thereafter let him bless the people thus: "The Lord bless you
and keep you! The Lord make his face shine upon you and be gracious unto you! The Lord lift up
his countenance upon you and give you peace! Amen." Or thus: "God be gracious and merciful unto
us and give us his divine blessing! May he cause his face to shine upon us and give us his
peace! Amen." Or thus: "Bless and keep us, God the Father and the Son and the Holy Ghost! Amen."
Or thus: "The blessing of God the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost be with you and
abide alway with us all! Amen."

The set passed, whole or shortened, into these orders:

| Order | Sehling | Forms offered |
|---|---|---|
| Brandenburg-Nürnberg 1533 | 11, p. 198 | Aaronic; Ps 67; "Gesegne und behüte uns, Gott der Vater …"; "Der Segen Gottes des Vaters …" |
| Pfalz-Neuburg 1543 | 13, p. 76 | the same four |
| Schwäbisch Hall 1543 | 17/1, p. 135 | Aaronic; the two Trinitarian forms |
| Strasbourg 1553 (Marbach) | 20/1, p. 415 | the same four |
| Württemberg 1553 | 16, p. 255 | Aaronic (singular); Ps 67; "Gesegne und behüte uns, Gott …" |
| Kurpfalz 1556 | 14, p. 150 | as Württemberg, in the plural |
| Worms, *Agendbüchlein*, 1560 | 19/1, p. 210 | the same four |
| Leiningen-Westerburg 1566 | 19/1, p. 253 | Aaronic; Ps 67; "Gesegne und behüte uns, Gott …" |
| Regensburg 1567 | 13, p. 463 | Aaronic (singular), or "Es segne und behüte euch Gott der Vater, Gott der Son und Gott der Heilig Geist" |
| Hanau-Lichtenberg 1573 | 20/2, p. 67 | Aaronic; Ps 67; "Gesegne und behüte uns, Gott …" |

**A Trinitarian blessing alone.** The first German Mass of Worms closed with a German
*Benedicamus* and the Trinitarian blessing only. **Worms, *Deutsche Messe*, 1524**
(Sehling 19/1, p. 129):

<!-- doc 1066 -->
> Last uns gott gebenedeien. Danck, lob, ere unnd preiß sei gott durch Christum, unsern herren,
> Amen. Benedictio: Der segen des Almechtigen gottes, vatters, suns und heyligen geyst, sei mit
> euch und bleib alweg bei euch. Amen.

Let us bless God. Thanks, laud, honour and praise be to God through Christ our Lord, Amen. The
blessing: "The blessing of Almighty God, Father, Son and Holy Ghost, be with you and abide alway
with you. Amen."

The Nunc dimittis followed. The two Strasbourg Masses of 1524 by Theobald Schwarz and in the
*Teutsche Meß und Tauff* end the same way: "Der segen Gott, des vatters [+] und des suns [+] und
des heiligen geists [+], sey mit uns und bleib alweg", with three signs of the cross
(Sehling 20/1, pp. 123, 133). At Regensburg in 1542 the priest blessed with the Trinitarian form
alone. **Regensburg, *Wahrhaftiger Bericht*, 1542** (Sehling 13, p. 393):

<!-- doc 432 -->
> Darnach kert sich der priester umb gegen dem volk und segnet dasselbig mit disen worten: Es
> gesegne und behüte uns Gott der Vater, Sone und Heiliger Geist. Amen

Thereafter the priest turneth about toward the people and blesseth them with these words: "Bless
and keep us God the Father, Son and Holy Ghost. Amen."

**By occasion: feasts and weekdays.** Prussia assigned a sung blessing from Psalm 67 to the
feasts, and the Aaronic blessing to ordinary days, at the end of matins, vespers and the Mass
alike. **Prussia, *Artikel der ceremonien und anderer kirchen ordnung*, 1525**
(Sehling 4, pp. 37–38):

<!-- doc 1833 -->
> Volgen die benediction oder beschluss gesang am encl der metten, vesper und mess. Diebus
> Festivis. Es wol uns gott seinen segen geben, unser got segen uns. [p. 38] […] Responsio. Und
> es sollen ihn ferchten alle ende der welt amen. Quotitiana benedictio. Der herr erleuchte sein
> angesicht über uns und sei uns gnedig

There follow the benediction or closing chant at the end of matins, vespers and Mass. On feast
days: "God give us his blessing, our God bless us." Response: "And all the ends of the world
shall fear him, Amen." The daily benediction: "The Lord make his face shine upon us and be
gracious unto us."

**By occasion: the sermon and the Supper.** Strasbourg in 1598 closed the plain preaching
service with the Trinitarian blessing alone, "Die Benedeiung Gottes, des Vatters, des Sohns und
des heiligen Geistes, seie jetzt und zu allen zeiten mit uns allen" (Sehling 20/1, p. 589). It
closed the Supper with the Aaronic and the Trinitarian together. **Strasbourg, *Kirchenordnung*,
1598** (Sehling 20/1, p. 613):

<!-- doc 1344 -->
> Der Herr segne Euch und behüte Euch. Der Herr laß sein Angesicht leuchten uber Euch und sei
> Euch gnädig. Der Herr hebe sein Angesicht uber Euch und geb Euch den Frieden. Die Benedeihung
> Gottes, des Vaters, des Sohns und des heil. Geistes, seie jetzt und zu allen zeiten mit uns
> allen, Amen. Gehet hin im Frieden des Herren

"The Lord bless you and keep you. The Lord make his face shine upon you and be gracious unto
you. The Lord lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace. The benediction of God, of
the Father, of the Son and of the Holy Ghost, be now and at all times with us all, Amen." Go
hence in the peace of the Lord.

**2 Corinthians 13:13.** The apostolic blessing, "Die gnad unsers Herrn Jesu Christi und die
liebe Gottes, sampt der gemeinschaft des heiligen Geistes", appears in two places:
- Hesse (1566) gives it as the alternative to Numbers 6 when the minister dismisses the
  congregation (Sehling 8, p. 248);
- Nördlingen (1579) closes the pulpit prayers with it (Sehling 12, p. 368).

**Psalm 67** stands beside the blessing in three forms:
- Luther's third option in the *Formula missae*;
- the second form of the Nürnberg set;
- the sung feast-day blessing of Prussia.

Where it was sung as Luther's hymn, "Es wolle uns Gott genädig sein", it closed the service
after the blessing: Nürnberg 1525 (Sehling 11, p. 57), Strasbourg 1525 (Sehling 20/1, p. 147),
and Hoya 1571, in a service without the Supper
(Sehling 6/2, p. 1152; [`HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md`](HYMN_PRACTICE_GUIDE.md), §15).

**After the blessing.**
- Pomerania (1535) ended there (Sehling 4, p. 341).
- Mecklenburg (1552) added "Erhalt uns, Herr, bei deinem Wort" or "Verleih uns Frieden"
  (Sehling 5, p. 199).
- Duke Henry's Saxony (1539) added "Gott sei gelobet", "and so go home" (Sehling 1, p. 275).

## 14. When nobody communicated

**The Mass without the Supper.** No evangelical order allowed a Mass without communicants. When
nobody had announced himself for the Supper, the Mass stopped after the sermon. What was left
was the first half of the ordo, closed with prayers. The postcommunion substitutes and the
Litany in this place are treated in [`COLLECTS_GUIDE.md`](COLLECTS_GUIDE.md), §6, and
[`EXTRAORDINARY_RITES_GUIDE.md`](EXTRAORDINARY_RITES_GUIDE.md), §22; the change of vestments in
[`CHURCH_DECORUM_GUIDE.md`](CHURCH_DECORUM_GUIDE.md), §7.

**The forms.**
- **Pomerania 1535**: "everything is sung, as is ordered, until the sermon; but after the sermon
  one or two German psalms" (Sehling 4, p. 341).
- **Mecklenburg 1552**: confession and absolution; a German psalm or two, or "Allein Gott";
  collect; epistle; psalm; gospel; *Patrem*; "Wir glauben"; the sermon with the prayer
  (Sehling 5, p. 199).
- **Albertine Saxony 1580**: "after the sermon, or a chapter of holy Scripture read, it shall be
  concluded with a Christian collect and the customary benediction" (Sehling 1, p. 369).
- **Transylvania 1547**: no Mass, but a German psalm, collect, sequence or tract, a chapter of
  the New Testament in course, "Wir glauben", and the suffrages.

The Transylvanian rubric shows how far the service fell back toward the office. **Transylvania,
*Kirchenordnung* (German), 1547** (Sehling 24, p. 244):

<!-- doc 1669 -->
> Wo aber zu diesem ampt keine communicanten vorhanden sein, wird keine meß nit gehalten, sonder
> an stat singt man zum ersten einen Deutschen psalmen, darauff die collect, meer ein
> Christliche sequentz oder tract oder was die zeit mit bringt. Darnach wird gelesen vom anfang
> des newen testaments biß zum ende nach der ordnung allweg ein Deutsch capitel, allein das man
> in der fasten die Deutsch episteln und evangelien behelt, wie sie nach den tagen verordnet
> sein. Nach der lection desselbigen capitels singt man: Wir gleuben all, mit dem versikel und
> collecten von der heiligen Dreifeltigkeit. Darnach folgen auch die andere suffragia, nemlich
> für den könig, umb vergebung der sunden und umb frieden mit iren versikeln und collecten,
> welches auch mit dem Benedicamus und segen des volcks beschlossen wirdt.

But where there are no communicants for this office, no Mass at all is held; but in its stead
there is sung first a German psalm, thereupon the collect, further a Christian sequence or
tract, or what the season bringeth. Thereafter is read, in order from the beginning of the New
Testament to the end, always one chapter in German; only that in Lent the German epistles and
gospels are kept, as they are appointed for the days. After the reading of the same chapter is
sung "We all believe", with the versicle and collect of the Holy Trinity. Thereafter follow also
the other suffrages, namely for the king, for the forgiveness of sins and for peace, with their
versicles and collects; which also is concluded with the *Benedicamus* and the blessing of the
people.

**The boys' daily Mass at Hof.** One order kept a Mass-shaped service with no communion at all,
as a school exercise. At Hof (1592) the boys' "*actus diurnus*" ran: the seasonal Kyrie and *Et
in terra* from the great missal; the gospel in Latin; sequence, tract or gradual; the gospel in
German; "the Sanctus of the season", with the Preface on the chief feasts; the Lord's Prayer
sung by a boy "in the deacon's place"; "the Agnus Dei of the season"; and a collect
(Sehling 11, pp. 472–473).
